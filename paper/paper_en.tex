\begin{abstract}
\kw{NinaXander} names a family of composed language models that connect frozen layers from different pretraining
families through a shared-latent adapter. We study the recurrent RWKV-4-Raven-7B and the Transformer-based
Tulu-Pythia-6.9b. An individual composed model is specified by its cross-family path and switch layer, as in \PtoRL{4}.
We test whether representations at corresponding layers can be connected by a comparatively lightweight adapter, and what effect that connection
has on the model's language performance. There are four main results.
First, under the matched conditions of a shared tokenizer, width, depth, and training
domain, the centered latent correlation reaches $\rho_{\mathrm{ctr}}{=}0.901$.
Shuffling the row correspondence destroys the alignment, yet the CKA of the raw
representations does not peak at same-numbered layers, and performance degrades sharply
under a domain shift to WikiText. The result is therefore not a general semantic space
but a token-aligned representation that is learnable under favorable conditions.
Second, the four-path read-outs are $0.695/0.559/0.711/0.590$ for
\RWKVself{}/\allowbreak\RtoP{}/\allowbreak\Pythiaself{}/\allowbreak\PtoR{}; the method uses no compression stage, yet
reconstruction error remains.
Third, the best direct affine maps reach $0.487/0.535$ for \RtoP{}/\allowbreak\PtoR{}, while
$13.4\%/12.0\%$ of the trained adapter's output remains unexplained by the respective best affine fits;
disabling the same adapter's nonlinear branch collapses both cross read-outs to negative
$\Rsq$.
Fourth, chimeras composed at a single boundary produce syntactically plausible
generations and perform multiple choice, but do not reach the stronger parent. \PtoR{}
exceeds \RtoP{} at deep boundaries by $8.9$--$18.8$ accuracy points at the same layer,
and the Pythia-prefix$\to$RWKV-suffix \PtoRL{4} keeps only $5$ Transformer layers,
cutting KV by $84.4\%$ while retaining ARC-Easy $62.2\%$ and SciQ $69.2\%$. It does not,
however, significantly exceed RWKV, and this choice of $L{=}4$ is itself a post-hoc
selection on the same two test sets. Generalization to out-of-domain WikiText also
remains a substantial problem.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Pretraining a language model demands enormous computation, yet its result remains a set of weights tied to one architecture.
Changing the model's inference characteristics may require reducing a KV cache that grows with context length. The usual
options are to pretrain a new hybrid that mixes recurrent and Transformer layers, or to convert or distill an existing
model by replacing sequence-mixing layers such as attention with a cheaper mechanism. Both approaches train new weights
inside the model. They do not recompose already-trained blocks as frozen components.

Existing approaches share this constraint. A hybrid trained from scratch pretrains the whole model, and the
conversion-and-distillation line retrains the sequence-mixing weights. Work that stitches frozen models together does exist, but
only with Transformers on both sides. Weight-space merging presupposes compatible weights. \kw{Recomposing finished, frozen
checkpoints as parts, across families,} remains open.

That is the idea this work tests. If the intermediate representations of independently trained heterogeneous models can be
translated into each other, existing checkpoints become \kw{frozen reusable parts}. Training one adapter makes many
composition points available at once, after which the accuracy--memory tradeoff can be searched \emph{post hoc}, without
per-configuration retraining. We use four switch positions in both directions, drawing eight configurations from one
adapter and computing their non-dominated set. The adapter itself still requires substantial training. Section~\ref{sec:repro}
gives the training configuration. This study does not establish a total training-cost advantage over pretraining or
distilling a new hybrid.

As a first step, we build a family of \emph{chimera models} called
\kw{NinaXander}.\footnote{\emph{NinaXander} derives from the chimera in \emph{Fullmetal Alchemist}: the synthesis succeeds,
but does not reach what it was made from.} These models connect frozen blocks from different families through one trained
adapter. Section~\ref{sec:method} specifies each model by its cross-family path and switch layer. RWKV is a model family
whose linear-attention computation also admits recurrent execution, while Tulu-Pythia is an instruction-tuned Transformer.
We study RWKV-4-Raven-7B and Tulu-Pythia-6.9b under the favorable conditions of a shared tokenizer, width, and depth. We ask three
questions. Does cross-family composition work? How do function and KV cost vary with the composition point? What limits
the connection?

The result is not a competitive new model. No configuration surpasses the stronger parent. Instead, the experiments show
that frozen assets can be partly recomposed after training, and that imperfect alignment, reconstruction error, and domain
shift obstruct the result. The concrete gain is a configuration that retains only $5$ Transformer layers, cuts KV by
$84.4\%$, and preserves part of the multiple-choice accuracy.
This work makes four contributions: alignment measurement after removal of the common mode; a controlled comparison of
\RWKVself{}, \RtoP{}, \Pythiaself{}, and \PtoR{}; an intervention within the same mapping that measures adapter
nonlinearity on both cross-family paths; and measurements of accuracy, memory, domain generalization, and path asymmetry
for single-boundary \RtoP{} and \PtoR{} chimeras.

\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\subsection{The residual stream and what composition means}
An autoregressive language model updates a per-position
representation, the residual stream, layer by layer. In a model of width $d$, block $\ell$ has the form
$h^{(\ell+1)}=h^{(\ell)}+f_\ell(h^{(\ell)})$, with $f_\ell$ performing sequence mixing and channel mixing. Composing at a
depth boundary $L$ means running one model's blocks $0$ through $L$, then feeding the resulting $h^{(L+1)}$ to the other
model's block $L{+}1$. Even when width and depth agree, $h^{(L+1)}$ lives in the coordinate system that each model's own
training fixed, so handing one model's value to the other does not match the distribution the downstream weights expect.
Whether the composition is possible therefore reduces to whether the residuals of the two families can be translated into
each other.

\subsection{The asymmetry in inference cost}
The two families differ in how their inference cost grows. A layer with
softmax attention must retain the keys and values of every past token, so it needs a cache of $O(T)$ in the context
length $T$. A layer that can be executed as linear attention, or in an equivalent recurrent form, carries only a
fixed-size state and is therefore $O(1)$. This asymmetry is what makes the composition worth studying here: which family
sits upstream and which downstream decides how many Transformer layers remain, and with them how much cache must be kept.

\subsection{The scale of the residual}
Residual scale differs from layer to layer, and does not agree across families.
Comparing residuals across layers, and handing them over at a boundary, therefore both require standardization by
statistics estimated per layer. This is why the adapter in this work is wrapped in per-layer standardization and its
inverse. Section~\ref{sec:method} gives the exact operations.

\subsection{Model stitching}
Connecting the layers of $2$ frozen models through a trainable stitching layer was introduced by
Lenc and Vedaldi~\cite{10.1007/s11263-018-1098-y} to measure the \emph{equivalence} of representations. They splice the lower
layers of one frozen CNN onto the upper layers of another with a linear stitching layer and score the resulting
``Franken-CNN'' by the original task loss. Their experiments include pairs trained on ImageNet and MIT Places, as well as
pairs drawn from genuinely different architectures such as AlexNet, VGG-16, and ResNet-50.
Bansal et al.~\cite{3540261.3540279} revisited stitching as a probe for representation comparison. They varied the random
seed, training subset, training duration, width, and objective while retaining the same architecture, and identified
cross-architecture stitching as an open difficulty. Csisz\'arik et al.~\cite{NEURIPS2021_2cb274e6} use a single affine
stitching layer to match the intermediate representations of two trained networks. They argue that agreement should be
judged by the task performance of the stitched network rather than by a representation-similarity index alone, and show
that existing similarity indices do not always track task performance. Our separation of alignment from decodability
follows this view. These studies concern vision models. Our work instead joins the distinct RNN and Transformer families
through a shared latent representation that translates the residual once at a layer boundary.

\subsection{Composing frozen LLMs}
Recent work continues to compose frozen LLMs through trainable connections.
StitchLLM~\cite{hu-etal-2025-stitchllm} stitches blocks of Transformers of different scales with stitching layers and selects a
path at serving time, and BTS~\cite{zhang-etal-2025-bts} exchanges hidden states \emph{bidirectionally} between a frozen seed and
frozen experts branched from that seed, through Experts-into-Hub and Hub-into-Experts stitch layers alternating every few layers.
CALM~\cite{bansal2024llm} fuses the intermediate representations of 2 frozen Transformers via cross-attention, and
Manticore~\cite{roberts2025pretrained} runs Transformer and Mamba block sequences \emph{in parallel} behind projections,
mixes them by a learned convex combination, and fine-tunes the resulting hybrid end to end. Its embeddings and language-model
head are newly initialized.

A separate line of work translates activations between frozen models. Moschella et al.~\cite{moschella2023relative}
re-encode samples relative to fixed anchors and thereby absorb the transformation between independently trained latent
spaces without learning a translator. Maiorca et al.~\cite{3666122.3668540} estimate a map between two pretrained latent
spaces in closed form from a small set of semantic correspondences. The affine control in Table~\ref{tab:bench} is a
low-capacity direct-map baseline of this kind.

Model Alignment Search~\cite{grant2025model} maps activations
bidirectionally between a frozen recurrent network and a frozen Transformer, but as a measure of representational similarity
rather than to build a composed model. Chen et al.~\cite{chen2025transferring} map the residual streams of 2 frozen language models into each other
bidirectionally with an affine map at a single layer, and evaluate the next-token cross-entropy of the composite that runs
one model's prefix into the other's suffix. Their pairs are same-family Transformers sharing a tokenizer, and they identify
the cross-family setting as future work.

The Bicameral Model preprint~\cite{flamant2026bicameralmodelbidirectionalhiddenstate}
couples 2 frozen language models through a bidirectional translation network, but there each model still executes its own
full stack in parallel. The GoldFinch preprint~\cite{goldstein2024goldfinchhighperformancerwkvtransformer} stacks a Transformer on top of RWKV-6 at a single boundary and
compresses the KV cache by $756$--$2550\times$, but trains the model from scratch rather than composing pretrained parents.
Oozeer et al.~\cite{oozeer2025activation} learn activation maps between frozen LLMs of different families to transfer safety
interventions and steering vectors. Their use of an affine control is close to ours, but the transfer remains at one layer
and they do not run a composed model. Our affine control is fitted directly to paired residuals and therefore uses stronger
supervision than zero-shot relative representations.

Prior work therefore either places Transformers on both sides, fine-tunes the composed model as Manticore does, or has at
most one pretrained parent. Chen et al.~\cite{chen2025transferring} have already established the mechanism of a single
boundary, two directions, and frozen parents for same-family Transformer pairs that share a tokenizer. We do not claim that
mechanism as novel. We address the cross-family case that they left open and test whether the same connection works between
a linear-attention recurrent model and a Transformer. To our knowledge, no prior study has composed these two families at a
single boundary while freezing both parents and both language-model heads, then evaluated the result as a working generator.
We further report the KV-cache
reduction that this composition yields.

\subsection{Representation similarity}
Centered kernel alignment (CKA)~\cite{pmlr-v97-kornblith19a} and (SV/PW)CCA~\cite{3295222.3295356,3327345.3327475} are standard tools for measuring representational
similarity between networks. Our work uses them as auxiliary metrics for the all-layer comparison while placing its main
focus on \emph{functional, reconstruction-mediated} stitching. On cross-architecture universality, Wang et
al.~\cite{wang2025towards} report that sparse-autoencoder features and induction circuits are largely similar between
Transformers and Mamba. Their result provides prior evidence for our premise, although their recurrent family is Mamba
rather than RWKV.

\subsection{Model merging and knowledge fusion}
Weight-space methods such as model soups~\cite{pmlr-v162-wortsman22a}, TIES~\cite{3666122.3666432}, and evolutionary
merging~\cite{Akiba2025} presuppose \emph{identical or compatible} weights. FuseLLM~\cite{wan2024knowledge} fuses output
distributions and does not require weight compatibility,
but it needs token alignment across vocabularies and continued training of the target model. Our work is complementary in that it merges
no weights at all and instead translates the \emph{activation space} to recombine frozen blocks.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Target models}
We use the instruction-following models RWKV-4-Raven-7B and Tulu-Pythia-6.9b. We denote the former by model $A$ and
the latter by model $B$. Both models have $32$ blocks, hidden width $d{=}4096$, and the GPT-NeoX-20B tokenizer. The shared
tokenizer turns the same input into the same token sequence, so residuals at each token position can be paired directly.
Equal depths let us pair block $j$ with block $j$, and equal hidden widths make the vectors at the boundary dimensionally
compatible. We selected the pair deliberately because these three conditions facilitate composition. Model pairs with
different tokenizers, depths, or hidden widths are outside the scope of this study.

The base models were pretrained on The Pile~\cite{gao2020pile}, an English corpus assembled by EleutherAI. The Pile is an
$825$ GiB language-modeling corpus that combines $22$ data sources, including scholarly articles, web documents, books,
and source code. The base corpus is shared, but the subsequent instruction-tuning data and prompt formats differ.

RWKV~\cite{peng-etal-2023-rwkv} uses the WKV operator for linear-time sequence mixing in place of softmax dot-product
attention and can execute the same computation in either parallel or recurrent form. Raven is an instruction-following
RWKV-4 model developed by the RWKV project and released by BlinkDL. It starts from RWKV-4-Pile-7B and receives further
training on general instruction datasets such as Alpaca, Guanaco, and GPT4All, as well as Code-Alpaca for code tasks and
ShareGPT for conversations. Code-Alpaca is a synthetic dataset of approximately $20{,}000$ instructions and responses,
mainly for code generation, editing, and optimization~\cite{codealpaca}. ShareGPT is a collection of multi-turn
conversations with ChatGPT that users shared publicly~\cite{lmsys-fastchat}.
We converted the Raven 7B weights distributed by BlinkDL to Hugging Face format and re-saved them in fp16 for this
study.\footnote{BlinkDL, RWKV-4 Raven model repository,
\url{https://huggingface.co/BlinkDL/rwkv-4-raven}.}
The weights we used contain $7{,}392{,}649{,}216$ parameters, with $32$ blocks, hidden width $4096$, and intermediate width
$16384$. The converted configuration sets \texttt{context\_length} to $1024$. This value is defined as the maximum sequence
length handled in one forward pass. The recurrent form can carry a fixed-size state whose size does not depend on context
length.\footnote{Hugging Face Transformers, RWKV documentation,
\url{https://huggingface.co/docs/transformers/model_doc/rwkv}.}
The filename and revision of the source \texttt{.pth} checkpoint were not preserved, so we cannot identify the training
context length claimed for that upstream checkpoint. Recurrent execution also does not guarantee language quality beyond
the length used in training or specified by the configuration.

Pythia~\cite{3618408.3618510} is a family of GPT-NeoX Transformers developed by EleutherAI for research.
Tulu-Pythia-6.9b is Pythia-6.9b fully fine-tuned by the Allen Institute for AI on the Tulu instruction mixture
~\cite{3666122.3669390}. The mixture includes FLAN V2, Chain-of-Thought, Dolly, OpenAssistant 1, GPT4-Alpaca,
Code-Alpaca, and ShareGPT.\footnote{Allen Institute for AI, \emph{open-instruct-pythia-6.9b-tulu} model card,
\url{https://huggingface.co/allenai/open-instruct-pythia-6.9b-tulu}.}
The weights we used contain $6{,}856{,}040{,}448$ parameters, with $32$ blocks, hidden width $4096$, and $32$ attention
heads. The configured maximum number of positions is $2048$.\footnote{The released configuration is available at
\url{https://huggingface.co/allenai/open-instruct-pythia-6.9b-tulu/blob/main/config.json}; its
\texttt{max\_position\_embeddings} value is $2048$.}
Based on the distributors' descriptions, Code-Alpaca and ShareGPT are the only instruction-tuning datasets named for both
models. The models were therefore not trained on completely independent data, although their architectures and most of
their instruction data differ.

\subsection{Residual pairs and notation}
In this paper, a \emph{residual} is not an error term. It is the hidden vector passed from one block to the next. Composing
the models means translating this vector from one model's coordinate system into the other model's coordinate system.
For model $M\in\{A,B\}$, let
$r_M^j[t]\in\mathbb R^{4096}$ denote the unstandardized residual after block $j$ at token position $t$. The block index is
$j\in\{0,\ldots,31\}$, and $t$ indexes a token in the input. We feed the same token sequence to both models and treat
$r_A^j[t]$ and $r_B^j[t]$ as one paired training example.

Residual scale differs by model and layer, so we estimate a per-dimension mean and standard deviation from the training
residuals of each layer. The operation $\mathrm{std}_M^j$ standardizes a residual with these statistics, while
$\mathrm{unstd}_M^j$ reverses that operation and restores the original scale. We write the standardized residual as
$x_M^j[t]=\mathrm{std}_M^j(r_M^j[t])$. For readability, the training objective below suppresses $j$ and $t$ and writes
these vectors as $x_A$ and $x_B$.

\subsection{Shared-latent adapter}
The adapter has one encoder and one decoder for each model. Encoders $E_A,E_B$ map standardized residuals into a shared
latent space, while decoders $D_A,D_B$ reconstruct each model's standardized residual from that space. Subscripts $A$ and
$B$ identify the model to which a map belongs. Vectors $z_A,z_B\in\mathbb R^{d_z}$ are the two latent representations
obtained at the same token position, and their latent width is $d_z{=}4096$. Let the hyperparameter $\lambda\geq0$ specify how strongly the latent-alignment error
is weighted relative to the two self-reconstruction errors. The training objective is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:adapter-objective}
\begin{aligned}
z_A &= \mathrm{LN}(E_A(x_A)),\qquad z_B = \mathrm{LN}(E_B(x_B)),\\
\mathcal{L} &=
\|D_A(z_A{+}\xi_A){-}x_A\|^2+\|D_B(z_B{+}\xi_B){-}x_B\|^2\\
&\quad+\lambda\|z_A{-}z_B\|^2 .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The first two terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:adapter-objective} are the self-reconstruction errors for models $A$ and $B$. The
third term is a latent-alignment error that brings $z_A$ and $z_B$ from the same token position closer together. Increasing
$\lambda$ increases the relative weight of this third term in the training loss $\mathcal L$. Each norm is implemented as
a mean squared error over all tokens and dimensions. Here $\mathrm{LN}$ is LayerNorm without a trainable scale or bias.
The vectors $\xi_A,\xi_B\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2 I)$ are independent, zero-mean Gaussian noise added only before decoding;
$\sigma>0$ is the noise-standard-deviation hyperparameter, and $I$ is the $d_z{\times}d_z$ identity matrix. Throughout
adapter training, we fix $\lambda{=}1$ and $\sigma{=}0.4$. Setting $\lambda{=}1$ gives the three mean-squared-error terms
the same nominal coefficient of $1$; it does not imply that their measured magnitudes are equal. Neither value is tuned by
layer, path, or task, and we do not ablate either value.

The loss contains no term that directly predicts a residual of $B$ from a residual of $A$, or vice versa. The cross-family
map sends $z_A$ from encoder $A$ to decoder $B$ and reads out $D_B(z_A)$. The reverse direction reads out $D_A(z_B)$.
These maps can work only if the third term brings $z_A$ and $z_B$ into the same coordinate system. Because training still
uses paired residuals, we do not describe the absence of a direct cross-family target as ``unsupervised learning.''

All four encoders and decoders have the same architecture: a linear projection, $b$ pre-norm residual blocks, and a linear
projection to the output. The reported configuration has latent width $4096$, $b{=}1$, and internal width $4096$. The second
matrix in each residual block is \kw{zero-initialized}, so the block is initially the identity and \emph{$E$ and $D$ begin
learning from linear maps}. The non-affine LN between them means that the complete cross-family map is not linear even at
initialization. Zero initialization describes only the starting point and does not imply that the trained map is linear.
The two parents contain $14.25$B parameters in total, while the adapter contains $268.5$M, or $1.9\%$ of the parent total.
We use ``lightweight'' in this relative sense and to refer to the shallow mapping depth, not to claim that the absolute
parameter count is small.

The noise $\xi$ is introduced to prevent the latent from satisfying the alignment term with a token-independent constant
known as the \emph{common mode}. The design rationale is that a constant cannot evade the noise. We fix $\sigma{=}0.4$ and
do not perform an ablation of its effect.

\subsection{Four paths and chimera construction}
The four paths are \RWKVself{}, \RtoP{}, \Pythiaself{}, and \PtoR{}.
\RtoP{} and \PtoR{} are cross-family chimeras; \RWKVself{} and \Pythiaself{} are same-family reconstruction controls
that pass through the same boundary and adapter. The switch layer $L$ uses zero-based indexing and denotes the last block
executed by the prefix parent. Because block $0$ is included, the prefix executes $L{+}1$ blocks.
Here $h_A^L[t]$ is the unstandardized residual produced by block $L$ of $A$ at token position $t$. The symbol $h_B^L[t]$
denotes the analogous residual produced by block $L$ of $B$. A tilde marks a residual translated into the other model's
coordinate system. Using $x_A^L[t]=\mathrm{std}_A^L(h_A^L[t])$ and
$x_B^L[t]=\mathrm{std}_B^L(h_B^L[t])$, the two boundary maps are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:boundary}
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde h_B^L[t]
 &= \mathrm{unstd}_B^L\!\left(D_B\!\left(\mathrm{LN}(E_A(x_A^L[t]))\right)\right),\\
\widetilde h_A^L[t]
 &= \mathrm{unstd}_A^L\!\left(D_A\!\left(\mathrm{LN}(E_B(x_B^L[t]))\right)\right).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The translated residual $\widetilde h_B^L[t]$ enters block $L{+}1$ of $B$, while $\widetilde h_A^L[t]$ enters block
$L{+}1$ of $A$. No training-time noise is added in Eq.~\eqref{eq:boundary}. Execution has three stages: run blocks $0$
through $L$ of the prefix parent, translate the residual once, then run blocks $L{+}1$ through $31$ and the language-model
head of the suffix parent. Every chimera therefore executes $32$ blocks in total, divided into $L{+}1$ prefix blocks and
$31{-}L$ suffix blocks.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}\label{sec:common-mode}
We measure residual recovery with the coefficient of determination
$\Rsq=1-\mathrm{SSE}/\mathrm{SST}$. Here $\mathrm{SSE}$ is the sum of squared differences between predicted and target
residuals, and $\mathrm{SST}$ is the sum of squared target residuals after subtracting the per-dimension token mean.
A value of $1$ means perfect recovery, $0$ matches a predictor that always returns
the per-dimension mean, and a negative value is worse than that mean predictor. We evaluate both cross-family maps
$A\!\to\!B$ and $B\!\to\!A$, as well as the same-family round trips $A\!\to\!A$ and $B\!\to\!B$. The latter two control
for information lost by encoding and decoding before the additional effect of crossing model families.

We compute $\Rsq$ separately at each layer by pooling the held-out tokens and all $4096$ dimensions. Because
$\mathrm{SST}$ is the total variation after removing the token mean of each dimension, dimensions with greater variance contribute more. Latent
agreement is measured with centered correlation $\rho_{\mathrm{ctr}}$. This is the Pearson correlation obtained after
removing each dimension's token mean from $z_A$ and $z_B$ and flattening the resulting token-by-dimension arrays. Values
near $1$ mean that the two latents vary in the same direction, while $0$ means no linear correlation. We also measure the
fraction of latent energy that varies across tokens as
$f=\lVert z-\overline z\rVert^2/\lVert z\rVert^2$. Here $\overline z$ is the per-dimension token mean within a layer.
We compute $f$ separately for $A$ and $B$ and average the two values. Every metric is computed per layer, and we report the
simple mean over all $32$ layers.

We use two held-out sets. The training-time evaluation that supplies the headline values contains $1{,}000$ windows, or
$112{,}000$ tokens. The per-layer analysis and shuffle control use $400$ windows, or $44{,}800$ tokens. Every window has
$112$ tokens. Because the evaluation sets differ, the $32$-layer averages in the per-layer analysis, $0.561/0.592$, do not
exactly match the headline values, $0.559/0.590$. The fitted $R^2$ over all layer correspondences is an in-sample ridge
linear probe that is fitted and evaluated on the same $22{,}400$ rows for every layer pair.

We evaluate the language function of the chimeras in three ways. First, we provide English prompts verbatim and generate
samples by deterministic argmax at temperature $0$. Generation stops after at most $40$ new tokens or earlier only when
the model emits EOS. Second, we score every answer option in ARC-Easy and SciQ by log likelihood and measure the accuracy
of selecting the highest-scoring option. Third, we measure next-token cross-entropy on Alpaca and WikiText to compare
language modeling within and outside the training domain.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
We first test whether corresponding layers can be aligned and how accurately each model's residual can be recovered from
the resulting representation. We then isolate the adapter's nonlinearity and evaluate the generation, accuracy, and
inference memory of the composed chimeras.


\subsection{A Learnable Shared Representation Exists Between Corresponding Layers}\label{sec:space}
Aligning two $7$B instruction-following models that share a tokenizer, depth, and hidden width on
Alpaca~\cite{alpaca} instruction-formatted text lets an adapter with one residual block reach a centered latent correlation of
$\rho_{\mathrm{ctr}}\!=\!0.901$ on unseen text. In this paper we claim a shared representation on the grounds of the centered
correlation of $0.901$ together with the fact that each decoder can reconstruct residuals of its own family
at $A\!\to\!A\ 0.70$ and $B\!\to\!B\ 0.71$. Moreover, even the best \emph{linear} maps fitted directly to the two
cross-family tasks reach $A\!\to\!B\ 0.487$ and $B\!\to\!A\ 0.535$. These substantial linear baselines show that the
expressive power of a deep nonlinear map alone cannot explain most of the observed predictability.

However, \kw{same-index layer pairs are not intrinsically special}. Figure~\ref{fig:cka} compares raw residuals without an
adapter across all $32{\times}32$ layer pairs. Linear CKA is weak throughout, with a diagonal mean of $0.05$ and an
off-diagonal mean of $0.03$. Layer $j$ of $A$ is most similar to layer $j$ of $B$ in only $2$ of $32$ rows. Layer $0$ is
the most similar target in $14$ rows, and the final layer is the most similar target in $12$ rows. Same-index layers are
also unexceptional in direct linear regressability. Per-layer linear maps fitted to all layer pairs attain fitted $R^2$
values from $0.62$ to $0.94$. Their diagonal mean is $0.87$, compared with an off-diagonal mean of $0.84$. The same-numbered layer is best in only $3$ of
$32$ rows. Thus $\rho_{\mathrm{ctr}}\!=\!0.901$ is an alignment \emph{learned and recovered on the selected same-index
layer pairs}, not a correspondence intrinsic to matching layer numbers. We position this work as a case study that selects
same-index layers from a broader regressability that exists across arbitrary layer pairs.

\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_layer_cka_en}
\caption{All $32{\times}32$ layer correspondences of raw residuals. The left panel shows linear CKA and the right shows

The alignment is \emph{learnable}, but equal layer numbers do not constitute a natural correspondence.

All numbers below come from a single training run of the same configuration. The latent width is $d_z{=}d{=}4096$, all $32$ blocks
are paired, the adapter has $268.5$M parameters, and $\sigma{=}0.4$. A plateau schedule reduces the learning rate from
$10^{-3}$ to a floor of $10^{-5}$. The training signal consists of residuals from $2{,}075$ windows of the Alpaca training
split. Each window contains $112$ tokens, giving approximately $232$k independent token positions. Across all $32$ layers,
these residuals form $7.44$M pairs, and training iterates over this pair set about $228$ times. Reported values come from
iteration $415{,}000$, the deepest checkpoint at which held-out $A\!\to\!B$ is best.
The \PtoR{} cross-family map attains $B\!\to\!A\ 0.590$, slightly above
$A\!\to\!B\ 0.559$. Residuals on the RNN side are therefore slightly easier to recover from the Transformer latent.
Both cross-family maps fall below the same-family reconstructions $A\!\to\!A\ 0.695$ and $B\!\to\!B\ 0.711$.
Even with high latent alignment, error is already present in same-family reconstruction, and cross-family read-out adds
further loss. We do not isolate whether this error originates in the encoder, latent normalization, decoder, or
training-time noise.

A negative control confirms that the alignment and read-outs depend on a true token-by-token correspondence rather than
distributional overlap. We keep the row order of $A$ fixed and re-pair the residual rows of $B$ with one random
permutation. This collapses $\rho_{\mathrm{ctr}}$ from $0.901\!\to\!0.000$, $A\!\to\!B$ from
$0.561\!\to\!-0.592$, and $B\!\to\!A$ from $0.592\!\to\!-0.609$. The same-family maps $A\!\to\!A$ and
$B\!\to\!B$ are unchanged by construction. Neither cross-family read-out is therefore obtainable without the correct
token correspondence.

In the following, $z$ denotes either $z_A$ or $z_B$, and $\overline z$ denotes its per-dimension token mean within the
layer. The quantity $\rho_{\mathrm{raw}}$ is the Pearson correlation of $z_A$ and $z_B$ before subtracting these token
means. The quantity $\mathrm{align}$ is the mean squared distance between $z_A$ and $z_B$ represented by the third error
term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:adapter-objective}, before multiplication by $\lambda$. Let $f$ denote the fraction of the latent
that varies across tokens. Under layer normalization without elementwise gains, the raw and centered correlations decompose as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:common-mode}
\begin{aligned}
f &= \frac{\lVert z-\overline z\rVert^2}{\lVert z\rVert^2},\\
\rho_{\mathrm{raw}} &= (1-f)+f\,\rho_{\mathrm{ctr}},\\
\mathrm{align} &= 2f(1-\rho_{\mathrm{ctr}}).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The reported $f$ is the mean of the varying fractions computed separately for $A$ and $B$. Equation~\eqref{eq:common-mode}
holds exactly when the two fractions are equal and their common modes are parallel. At evaluation points after training has
progressed, the measured relation holds to a precision of about $3\!\times\!10^{-4}$. It does not hold early in training;
at iteration $0$, the residual reaches $6.6\!\times\!10^{-2}$. Equation~\eqref{eq:common-mode} shows that raw correlation
contains both centered correlation and the contribution of a constant term. The fraction $1-f$ comes from a constant that
carries no information. Since $f$ at the deepest point is $0.350$, \kw{$65\%$ of the latent's energy is a token-independent
constant}, and this constant dominates the raw correlation. The centered $\rho_{\mathrm{ctr}}$ is $0.901$, while the raw
correlation is $0.965$. Equation~\eqref{eq:common-mode} reproduces this value exactly as
$(1{-}0.350)+0.350\times0.901=0.965$.

\paragraph{What $f$ does and does not show}$f$ is a second moment, not a measure of information, and the inferences it licenses run in one direction
only. $f\!\to\!0$ proves that the latent has collapsed to a constant, and hence that it carries no per-token information at all.
A large $f$ means only that the per-token latents are spread out in angular direction;
under layer normalization without elementwise gains, $f$ is strictly invariant to scaling. Spread arises equally from meaningful variation and from meaningless
variation. It is therefore the read-out, not $f$, that can establish that the varying component carries information.
The read-out is a centered $\Rsq$, so no token-independent predictor can exceed $0$. The best constant scores exactly
$0$. Hence $B\!\to\!B=0.711$ cannot be obtained from any constant, which is consistent with $65\%$ of the latent's
energy being constant.

Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder} shows the five measured diagnostic values.
\[
 \begin{array}{ll}

 \Rsq(B\!\to\!B)=0.711, & \Rsq(A\!\to\!B)=0.559,\\
 \Rsq(A\!\to\!A)=0.695, & \Rsq(B\!\to\!A)=0.590 .
 \end{array}

The conclusions of this section rest on two observations. First, among the $\Rsq$ values,
$A\!\to\!B\le B\!\to\!B$ and $B\!\to\!A\le A\!\to\!A$ hold in all $32$ layers. Second, the loss from the encoding
and decoding round trip also appears in downstream performance. It is significant for \Pythiaself{} at every switch layer
on both tasks, and for \RWKVself{} at $L{=}16,24$ on both tasks.

High latent correlation does not guarantee high reconstruction accuracy. \RtoP{} can be compared with \Pythiaself{}, which passes through the same decoder $D_B$, and
\PtoR{} with \RWKVself{}, which passes through the same decoder $D_A$; the only difference is whether the input is a same-family or a cross-family latent.
Both inequalities are empirical observations rather than theorems, but in either direction a substantial error is already present at the self-reconstruction stage,
and improving only the cross-family step leaves that error in place.

At $d_z{=}d{=}4096$ the latent is as wide as the residual. The non-affine LN discards only two degrees of freedom per token,
namely its mean and norm, so width or rank is unlikely to be a strong constraint on reconstruction. Even so,
$B\!\to\!B$ remains at $0.711$. Whether this is a ceiling set by the noise $\sigma$ is unknown, and what is rate-limiting
remains an open problem.

\subsection{The Adapter Is Nonlinear on Both Cross Paths}\label{sec:linear}
Each of the adapter's $E$ and $D$ is a single zero-initialized residual block sandwiched between two linear maps, so each begins training as a linear map. But that is a property before training,
not after $415{,}000$ iterations. We measured how linear the adapter at the deepest point actually is,
for both \RtoP{} and \PtoR{}, on the same $5$ representative layers and the same fit and held-out rows.

The best affine map mimicking the adapter's own output explains
$86.6\%$ of the output variance for \RtoP{} and $88.0\%$ for \PtoR{}. Hence \kw{the remaining $13.4\%$/$12.0\%$
of the output variance cannot be captured by the fitted affine maps}. The trained map is clearly nonlinear on both paths.

The comparison is not an approximation of the adapter: it is a control obtained by \emph{directly} fitting,
per layer and separately for each path, the best affine map from the standardized input residuals to the standardized output residuals via ridge least squares.
Evaluated on the held-out rows of the $5$ representative layers $\{4,10,16,22,28\}$,
the best direct affine map reaches $0.487$ for \RtoP{} and $0.535$ for \PtoR{}. The corresponding adapter, on the same rows, achieves $0.558$ for \RtoP{} and $0.576$ for \PtoR{},
so the nonlinear map's increment is $+0.071$/$+0.041$. Because even a low-capacity affine map achieves substantial
cross-family prediction on both paths, \emph{the shared-representation claim is underwritten by this linear reachability}.

\emph{The separately fitted affine control.} At each switch layer this control is fitted by ridge regression with an
intercept and a regularization coefficient of $10^{-3}$. The fit uses $40{,}096$ rows of standardized residuals drawn from the held-out region of Alpaca that the adapter
was not trained on. In Table~\ref{tab:bench}, replacing the \RtoP{} adapter with the direct affine map
collapses accuracy by $8$--$23$ points. For \PtoR{}, by contrast, the gap is small, and on SciQ @8 the affine control is $3.4$ points higher.
This matters as a path difference, but because the two maps differ in training objective, data, and regularization, we do not treat it as a causal effect of nonlinearity.

collapses representation and downstream accuracy alike on both \RtoP{} and \PtoR{}. This, however, is a property of removing the
branch alone from a jointly trained map; it does not show that nonlinearity is generally necessary for connecting the families.
At least for \PtoR{} accuracy, the separately fitted affine control differs little from the adapter. The difference ranges
from $-3.4$ to $+2.0$ points across the $8$ cells, and Table~\ref{tab:bench} shows that the adapter does not consistently
outperform it.

\subsection{Syntactically Plausible Generation, Degraded Factuality}\label{sec:chimera}

for a total of $L{+}1$ blocks. We translate its residual \emph{once} into the other space with Eq.~\eqref{eq:boundary}, then
run the suffix parent's blocks from $L{+}1$ onward together with its language-model head. This is not a per-block round trip.

\subsection{Function and Inference-Memory Comparison Against the Parents}\label{sec:goal}
Assessing the practical value of NinaXander requires a functional comparison against the frozen parents, not internal-representation metrics alone.
We measure three aspects: how much performance is retained relative to the stronger parent, whether the weaker parent is
surpassed, and how much KV cache is saved in exchange.

This comparison is run in a multiple-choice format that computes the log-likelihood of each option and selects the maximum. Because no generation, string matching, or subjective judgment
is involved, the result is uniquely determined. We use the \kw{full evaluation sets}: the ARC-Easy test set contains
$2376$ questions~\cite{clark2018thinksolvedquestionanswering}, and the SciQ test set contains $1000$
questions~\cite{welbl-etal-2017-crowdsourcing}. We evaluate the $d_z{=}4096$ adapter at its deepest reported point,
iteration $415{,}000$, at four switch positions. Between-configuration comparisons on the same question set are paired
data, so differences are assessed with \kw{paired tests}. We use McNemar's exact test and paired bootstrap rather than
independent per-cell binomial standard errors. Because the options differ in
length and sum vs.\ length-normalized scoring can disagree in ranking, both are stored in the structured data, and the main text uses length-normalized scoring for reporting and testing.
Moreover, SciQ is evaluated in a closed-book format that supplies only the question and answer options, not the accompanying support passages. The SciQ values in this paper
are not directly comparable to published values under the standard protocol that provides support.

Six observations follow from the joint comparison of the four paths. First, \kw{even with the prefix and suffix from different families, both \RtoP{} and \PtoR{} can actually
answer the questions}. All cross configurations far exceed chance, reaching \RtoP{} $70.9\%$ and \PtoR{} $69.9\%$ on SciQ.
Second, \kw{the depth effect is path-dependent}. \RtoP{} decreases monotonically over $L=4\to24$, SciQ
$70.9\to67.7\to54.5\to51.1$ and ARC-Easy $59.6\to59.0\to48.6\to47.0$, whereas
\PtoR{} is non-monotonic, SciQ $69.2\to64.6\to68.0\to69.9$ and ARC-Easy $62.2\to57.3\to57.5\to59.6$.
Local read-out at the same four layers is \RtoP{} $0.570/0.555/0.575/0.567$ and \PtoR{} $0.552/0.517/0.604/0.608$,
so the switch layer or $R^2$ alone does not determine the ranking.
Third, \kw{the stronger parent is not surpassed on either path}. All $8$ cross configurations fall below Tulu-Pythia on both tasks,
and the differences are significant under McNemar tests on the same questions in every case.
Fourth, \kw{the difference against RWKV is likewise path- and task-dependent}. \RtoPL{4} exceeds RWKV by $+3.9$ points on SciQ with
$p{=}0.018$, but is $-2.2$ points on ARC-Easy with $p{=}0.042$. Under sum scoring, this advantage disappears.
The sum-score difference for \RtoPL{4} is $-2.7$ points on SciQ, while the differences for \PtoRL{4} are $-4.6$ points
on ARC and $-5.7$ points on SciQ. The verdict against the weaker parent therefore depends on the choice of length
normalization. \PtoR{} does not significantly exceed RWKV at any boundary. \PtoRL{4} is statistically equivalent at
ARC with a difference of $+0.4$ points and $p{=}0.66$, and at SciQ with a difference of $+2.2$ points and $p{=}0.14$.
Fifth, \kw{the difference against the separately fitted affine controls is also path-dependent}. On \RtoP{} the adapter is $8$--$23$ points higher in every cell,
whereas on \PtoR{} the differences are small, and at SciQ @8 the affine is $3.4$ points higher. That is, the trained adapter does not always outperform an affine mapping.
Sixth, \kw{the gap to the same-family controls is not translation error alone}. With the B suffix, accuracy decreases in the order pure Pythia, \Pythiaself{}, \RtoP{},
with \Pythiaself{}--\RtoP{} gaps of SciQ $4$--$19$ points and ARC $3$--$12$ points. With the A suffix, by contrast, \PtoR{} conversely exceeds \RWKVself{} at deep boundaries,
and the differences at $L=16/24$ are ARC $+2.7/+7.4$ points and SciQ $+8.2/+12.0$ points.
The same-family controls isolate the loss from the encode--decode round trip, but because the gap to the cross paths contains both the prefix parent's capability and translation mismatch,
it cannot be read as a universal ``translation cost.''

\paragraph{Depth dependence differs between \RtoP{} and \PtoR{}}As Table~\ref{tab:direction} shows, at $L{=}16,24$ \PtoR{} exceeds the same-layer \RtoP{} by
$+8.9/+12.6$ points on ARC and $+13.5/+18.8$ points on SciQ. McNemar tests on the same questions give
$p<3{\times}10^{-15}$ in every case. At $L{=}4$, on the other hand, the difference is $+2.6$ points on ARC with
$p{=}0.015$, but $-1.7$ points on SciQ with $p{=}0.28$. The path difference is therefore not uniform at shallow
boundaries. Every \PtoR{} configuration falls significantly below Pythia,
and no \PtoR{} configuration significantly exceeds RWKV;
\PtoRL{4} is equivalent at ARC $62.2$ vs.\ $61.8$ with $p{=}0.66$ and SciQ $69.2$ vs.\ $67.0$ with $p{=}0.14$.

The best affine mapping for \PtoR{} is a control fitted separately from the adapter at each switch layer. At ARC @4 the adapter is $+2.0$ points higher
with $p{=}0.005$, but most of the other cells show no difference. At SciQ @8 the affine is conversely $+3.4$ points higher
with $p{=}0.003$.
Separately from this between-mapping comparison, the intervention that sets the nonlinear branch of the same adapter to $\alpha=0$ is executed for \RtoP{}/\allowbreak\PtoR{} at the same four layers and the same questions,
and the causal contribution of the nonlinear branch is assessed from those paired results. The comparison with the \RWKVself{} control likewise
replaces the RWKV prefix simultaneously with a Pythia prefix and cross translation, and thus does not isolate translation error alone.

\paragraph{The memory effect of composition: the KV cache disappears together with the Transformer layers}Performance and inference memory must be evaluated jointly,
because the two families do not have the same inference-time cost. Transformer layers cache key/value per token,

and \PtoR{} only $L{+}1$. What is reduced is the KV cache, not the resident weights: the adapter itself occupies $0.50$ GiB in fp16, or about $0.25$ GiB if only one direction is kept resident, which is comparable to the reduced KV.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
In our experiments, a shared representation with $\rho_{\mathrm{ctr}}\!=\!0.90$ can be learned between corresponding
layers of frozen heterogeneous families. \kw{However, the scope of this claim is limited}. We measure the correspondence
under favorable conditions: an identical tokenizer, width, layer count, and input, together with a domain close to Alpaca.
The shuffled control rules out token-independent distribution matching, but we do not disentangle whether the observed
correspondence stems from semantics, vocabulary, position, or syntax. Section~\ref{sec:goal} shows that perplexity degrades
on out-of-domain WikiText. This result leaves generalization beyond the training domain in question. We therefore do not
claim a ``general shared \emph{semantic} space.'' Our claim is limited to a shared space of token-aligned representations
that is learnable under favorable conditions.

A functioning composition does not establish that the two sides represent the same information. Smith et
al.~\cite{pmlr-v267-smith25a} successfully stitch across different tasks and modalities, and even from representations
derived from clustered random noise. Their stitching layers are trained on downstream task loss. Balogh and
Jelasity~\cite{balogh2025not} show that this regime rates non-corresponding and out-of-distribution representations highly.
Our adapter instead learns by directly matching corresponding residuals, which the same study finds balances structural
and functional requirements. Even so, Smith et al.\ obtain spurious alignment with a low-capacity stitch, while our adapter
is considerably more expressive. Section~\ref{sec:linear} shows that a low-capacity affine map already achieves substantial
cross-family prediction. This result is our main reason for concluding that adapter capacity alone does not manufacture the
observed alignment.

High latent correlation also does not guarantee high read-out accuracy. The presence of a shared representation and its
decodability must be evaluated separately. Zhang and Xin independently report the same distinction in their
preprint~\cite{zhang2026negativeresultcrossmodelactivation}. Their linear bridge between Pythia models reaches a normalized
$R^2$ of $0.883$ offline, yet degrades multi-hop reasoning when its mapped activation replaces the receiver's activation.
They conclude that the objective must include causal use by the receiver. Our adapter is likewise trained only on
activation reconstruction and falls within the scope of this criticism. Equation~\eqref{eq:boundary} does include per-layer
inverse standardization, which restores the receiver's native scale, whereas their bridge trains under a normalized
objective. We do not isolate whether this structural difference is what matters. A chimera built on this space functions,
but at a loss, and in the generation samples both paths remain fluent
while erring in content or form. A competitive chimera requires identifying the factors that bottleneck $B\!\to\!B$, training across domains, and selecting complementary parent models on the pretraining side.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
The evaluation tasks are exposed. Tulu-Pythia is instruction-tuned on a subset of FLAN V2, whose task inventory includes
ARC and SciQ. Verbatim matching against the released components finds $19$ ARC-Easy and $4$ SciQ evaluation items. The
matching procedure cannot detect paraphrases or formatting changes. It also excludes questions shorter than $6$ words to
avoid chance collisions; this exclusion covers $40$ ARC-Easy and $11$ SciQ questions. The detected counts are therefore
lower bounds. Removing the matched items from the per-item correctness records changes no configuration's accuracy by more
than $0.22$ points and changes none of the comparisons in this paper. The same items also become harder or easier in the
same direction for RWKV, which had no such exposure. Its change is $-9.2$ points on ARC and $+8.0$ points on SciQ, so the
gap reflects item difficulty rather than exposure. Nevertheless, ARC-Easy and SciQ remain task families already present in
the suffix parent's training. Our values should be read as capability retained through composition, not capability newly
acquired.

Adapter training uses only a single random seed. Performance is measured on the \kw{full evaluation sets}, which contain
$2376$ ARC-Easy questions and $1000$ SciQ questions. Differences between configurations were assessed on the same
questions with McNemar's exact test and paired bootstrap. \RtoP{} exceeds the weaker parent only on SciQ and only under
the length-normalized score. The advantage does not hold under the sum score. \PtoR{} does not significantly exceed RWKV
at any boundary. We evaluate $2$ tasks $\times2$ cross paths $\times4$ switches plus multiple controls, without
correction for multiple comparisons.
The identical-adapter intervention on the nonlinear branch was performed at the same layers and questions for both \RtoP{}/\allowbreak\PtoR{}, but it is an intervention on a single checkpoint
and may not generalize to different adapter training conditions.

Moreover, the composition always has only a single boundary, and we did not measure how translation error accumulates across multiple boundaries. Furthermore, the chimera evaluation is
likelihood-based, which measures the ability to \emph{select} the correct option and does not directly measure the ability to \emph{generate}.

The four generation samples for each of \RtoP{} and \PtoR{} are only a qualitative supplement, not a comprehensive
evaluation of generation ability. In addition, the translation is \emph{specialized to the training domain} and degrades
substantially on WikiText. Long-context cross-entropy is measured at contexts $512$, $1024$, and $2048$.
Tulu-Pythia has a configured maximum of $2048$ positions, and both cross-family paths include this model, so we use $2048$
as their common evaluation limit. Measurements beyond the converted RWKV configuration's value of $1024$ are extrapolations
beyond its configured length. We also cannot recover the upstream training context claimed by BlinkDL because the source
filename and revision were not retained. These measurements concern language-modeling quality and do not directly measure
reading comprehension that requires long-range dependencies. Ablations over $\sigma$, $\lambda$, latent width, the number
of residual blocks, and the amount of training data, as well as alignment on a same-family pair with an identical
architecture, were not conducted in this $32$-layer configuration and
are left for future work.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
This work built \kw{NinaXander}, a family of chimera models that connect frozen blocks from different pretraining
families at a single boundary through one shared-latent adapter, and measured on RWKV-4-Raven-7B and
Tulu-Pythia-6.9b what does and does not hold for cross-family composition, from the level of the representation down
to downstream function. Under the favorable conditions of a shared tokenizer, width, depth, and training domain, the
alignment between corresponding layers is \emph{learnable}: the centered latent correlation reaches $0.901$, while
shuffling the row correspondence collapses it to $0.000$. Since the CKA of the raw representations does not peak at
same-numbered layers and performance degrades out of domain, what is shown is not a general semantic space but a
token-aligned representation learnable under favorable conditions. Error is already present at the same-family
reconstruction stage, before the cross-family step. The cross read-outs fall below, in every layer, the same-family
controls that use the same respective decoders, and we do not isolate whether that
error originates in the encoder, the latent normalization, the decoder, or the training-time noise. Removing the
nonlinear branch from the trained adapter collapses both cross read-outs to negative $\Rsq$, but that is a property of
removing the branch alone from a jointly trained map and does not show that nonlinearity is generally necessary for
connecting the families; for \PtoR{}, indeed, the separately fitted affine control differs little from the adapter.
On the functional side, training the adapter once yields $8$ configurations ($4$ switch positions $\times$ $2$
directions) without per-configuration retraining, and the composed chimeras produce syntactically plausible
generations and perform multiple choice. \PtoRL{4} keeps only $5$ Transformer layers, cutting the KV cache by
$84.4\%$ while retaining ARC-Easy $62.2\%$ and SciQ $69.2\%$. What is reduced, however, is the KV cache and not the
resident weights: the adapter itself occupies $0.50$ GiB in fp16, or about $0.25$ GiB if only one direction is kept
resident, which adds a resident cost comparable to the reduced KV of $0.31$ GiB at a $4$k context. In the generation
samples both paths remain fluent while erring in content or form, and no configuration surpasses the stronger parent
Tulu-Pythia. \PtoRL{4} does not significantly exceed RWKV either: it is statistically equivalent at ARC $62.2$ vs.\
$61.8$ ($p{=}0.66$) and SciQ $69.2$ vs.\ $67.0$ ($p{=}0.14$). The default $L{=}4$ is itself a post-hoc selection on
the reported tests, with no independent deployment-selection split.

The open problems follow from the same measurements. The largest weakness is generalization out of domain: at context
$2048$, \PtoRL{4} has a WikiText perplexity of $34.78$, still far from the $8.27$ of the better parent RWKV.
Alongside it remain identifying what limits $B\!\to\!B$ at $0.711$, training across domains, selecting
complementary parent models on the pretraining side, measuring how translation error accumulates across multiple
boundaries, ablations over $\sigma$, $\lambda$, the latent width, the number of residual blocks, and the amount of
training data, and reproduction across several random seeds. What this work shows is not a competitive new model but
that post-hoc recomposition of frozen assets is partly possible and that additional trade-off points can be created
between the frozen parents; describing the conditions and limits of that route as measurements is the contribution of
this paper.
